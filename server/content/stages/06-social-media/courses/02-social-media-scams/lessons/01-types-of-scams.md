## Common Schemes

**A hacked friend's account:** a message reading "I urgently need money, send it to my card" from someone you know, sent from an account that's been taken over. **Fake giveaways:** "Share and win an iPhone!" -- a scheme built to inflate a page's shares and followers rather than give anything away. **Fake charity fundraisers** exploiting real disasters or causes. **Phishing via direct message:** "Click this link, you've won!" leading to a credential-harvesting page. **Prepaid job offers:** "Work from home, but pay for training first." **Investment pyramids:** "Invest $100, get $1,000 back" -- often shared through WhatsApp groups full of supposed "successful investors" posting fabricated testimonials.

## How to Check

Call your friend directly, through a different channel than the one the request arrived on, before sending money based on a social media message. Check whether a profile is authentic -- a recently created account, a mismatched name and photo, or an unusually small friend count are all signs of impersonation. Don't click links inside unsolicited direct messages, and never send money to someone you've only interacted with online, no matter how long the relationship has felt real. Report and block suspicious accounts rather than replying to them, since replying often only confirms to a scammer that your account is active and worth continued effort. None of these checks take more than a minute or two, which is a small cost against the alternative of losing money that's rarely recoverable once sent.

## Real-world case: the platform where scams cost people the most {#case}

The Federal Trade Commission's 2025 fraud data shows just how concentrated social media scam losses have become. Nearly 30% of people who reported losing money to any kind of scam in 2025 said it started on social media, with total reported losses reaching $2.1 billion -- eight times higher than in 2020. Investment scams accounted for the largest share by far: $1.1 billion, more than half of the total, often starting with an ad or post promising to teach followers how to invest, or a WhatsApp group full of fabricated "successful investor" testimonials. Romance scams were a distant second at $298 million, with scammers tailoring a fake relationship to a target's profile before inventing a financial crisis or steering the relationship toward a fake investment platform. Facebook accounted for more reported losses than any other platform, with WhatsApp and Instagram a distant second and third ([FTC data spotlight, April 2026](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/data-visualizations/data-spotlight/2026/04/reported-losses-scams-social-media-eight-times-higher-2020)). The concentration in one category -- investment scams -- and one platform -- Facebook -- suggests scammers are following where the largest, most persuadable audiences already are, rather than spreading effort evenly.
