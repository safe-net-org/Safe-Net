## How to request feedback

1. Start with **one professor**, then adapt later messages to the recipient's actual work. Avoid sending the same presentation to a large list in one batch.
2. Identify yourself accurately as a high-school student and the builder of a working prototype. Describe the exact parts you personally implemented; credit collaborators, open-source dependencies and AI assistance where relevant.
3. Keep the first email roughly 120–180 words. Ask one question from the table. Request a brief comment or a suggested reading, rather than a meeting, recommendation letter or mentorship commitment.
4. Include a concise PDF and repository link. A local `localhost` address will not work for the recipient. If no public demo exists, provide a short recorded walkthrough instead.
5. State the evidence boundary plainly: local engineering tests and a demonstrated course flow do not prove that the platform improves real-world phishing resistance. No user study or external validation should be implied unless actually completed.
6. Do not ask a professor to install Guard to understand the project. Supply screenshots, permission/data-flow explanation and a video. Installation should remain an optional, clearly explained action.
7. A single polite follow-up after approximately 10–14 days is reasonable as a practical outreach convention; no response is not permission to keep contacting them. These timing suggestions are etiquette recommendations, not published university policies.
8. Keep a private record of date contacted, exact question, response and resulting project change. Do not publish a professor's reply, name as an advisor, or implied endorsement without their permission.

## What makes this a strong activity

Document a specific question, the feedback received, the change you made and how you evaluated it. That record shows what the project gained from the exchange. The number of people contacted says little about the outcome; a reply alone does not establish a collaboration or an admissions advantage.

A useful next deliverable would be a short, consent-based evaluation protocol with unfamiliar examples, legitimate examples as controls, a delayed check and clear privacy boundaries. Seek feedback on that protocol before recruiting participants. If an institution becomes involved, ask its faculty member what review and consent procedures apply, especially for participants who are minors. Use simulated examples for the demonstration, without collecting credentials or exposing participants to real malicious sites.
